## Iteration 6 — The head spring

Date: 2026-09-18. Goal: make a charge visible on Richie himself — the head sinks while Space is held and pops out on the hop, to a limit, still attached. AI/tool: Claude Code. Generated: a damped spring on the head node's local Y (k=90, c=9, sub-stepped at 20 ms so a slow frame cannot blow it up), a target of −45 mm scaled by the charge while Space is down, an upward impulse on every launch (hop, Voxxy throw, Biggy hit), and hard stops at −45/+50 mm. Observed problem: the headless software renderer runs this scene at a few frames a second, so live samples could not resolve the pop; the spring was verified by simulating the same maths at 60 fps (peak +49.5 mm at 0.15 s, settled by 0.8 s) and the dip by a close-up render. Result: charging reads on the robot, not just the bar.

## Iteration 10 — Reference-sheet robots, a textured Kinepolis

Date: 2026-09-18. Goal: "hyper realism" — the robots rebuilt from the Robot Games model sheets, and the venue dressed with generated textures: carpet, booths selling absurd robot gadgets, Java's Duke. AI/tools: Claude Code for the models, the venue and the pipeline; ChatGPT image generation for the textures. Prompts: prompts/002-textures.md. Generated: `src/robots.ts` rewritten against the three sheets (measured off the front and profile views, open beside the game); `src/kit.ts`, a merge-by-material modelling kit; `src/textures.ts` and `scripts/import-texture.py`; `src/venue.ts` — booths, reception, ceilings, walls, fittings, seats, stage; zone light grading and a bloom pass in `src/main.ts`; sixteen generated images, driven through the author's own ChatGPT session by a small in-page queue that submits a prompt, waits for the render and saves it under a fixed name.

Observed problems: the previous robots matched the sheets' descriptions but not the sheets — Biggy was a box on four legs with hazard stripes, where the sheet shows a rusting orange ball under a riveted dome on two stubby legs; Droid had a box head with a sensor bar instead of a dome with two round eyes. The first ChatGPT session turned out to be in a browser on another laptop, so its downloads landed on the wrong machine; the conversation was reopened from the right one. Generated "seamless" textures are not: the import step cross-fades each against a half-offset copy. Keying Duke's white background by luminance would have punched holes in his white belly; a flood-fill from the borders does not. The standee arrived wearing a trademarked coffee-cup logo on its badge, which the import step paints over. With bloom on, most of the frame went black: not overflowing highlights, as first assumed, but NaN — Richie's decimated mesh carries a few dozen zero-length normals, each one a single black pixel nobody had noticed until a blur smeared it across the screen. It was found by reading the bloom's bright-pass target back as raw half-floats and locating the NaN under Richie. Ceiling pieces cast a slab of shadow across the hall until fittings got a kit of their own. The sign textures had never been tagged sRGB, so every sign in the game was washed out. The old seats had their backs on the stage side, in front of the audience's knees. Human decisions: use the reference cards for the robots and ChatGPT for Kinepolis textures; make the booths absurd. Changes: as above; booth colliders now follow the booth (back wall, side wall, counter) instead of a solid block, so Richie can hop into a stand. Result: a venue that reads as Kinepolis in every zone — grey exhibition carpet under an orange cove, a stone staircase with lit nosings, confetti carpet upstairs, a dark room of red velvet facing a lit screen — and three robots you could hold up against their model sheets. The first full capture run then timed out on its last step: on a software renderer the scene cost 1.2 s a frame, and the bloom pass turned out to be only a tenth of that — the rest was triangles. A per-mesh count found 892k, of which about 300k were new: 90k in 150 rounded velvet seats, 190k in seven full-detail robots (the corridor minis are the same models), 17k in popcorn. Coarser corner rounding, fewer radial segments and twenty-triangle kernels brought it to 760k with nothing visibly lost, and the capture script's per-step budget went from three to eight minutes. Verified: type-check, unit tests, production build, and the release capture script end to end. Next: texture the crowd's world a little less kindly than the robots'; consider KTX2 compression if the 4.5 MB of images hurts first load.

## Iteration 12 — A way out

Date: 2026-09-18. Goal: let a player leave a run, on a laptop with Escape. AI/tool: Claude Code. Generated: a paused state with a card that resumes or quits, a MENU button in the HUD for devices without a keyboard, BACK TO MENU on the results card, and `pause()`/`menu()` on the debug hook. Human decision: a pause card rather than an immediate exit, so Escape cannot throw away a ten-minute run by accident. Considered: the pause gates the physics step, not the render loop, so the building carries on behind the card — but that meant the guards, who update in the render loop, could still walk over and grab Richie through it; they now stop seeing him while the card is up, and the keynote clock, which only advances inside a physics step, stops on its own. Escape in fullscreen also leaves fullscreen, which is the browser's to decide and is left alone. Result: a run can be left and restarted without reloading the page.

## Iteration 13 — The escalators run

Date: 2026-09-18. Goal: the escalators beside the grand staircase should work, not decorate. AI/tool: Claude Code. Generated: generated tread and handrail textures that scroll along the ramp, comb plates at both ends, and a carry in the physics step that takes over Richie's velocity along the slope while he is on the treads. Considered: animating step geometry, which would have meant a moving collider for a ramp Richie has to stand on; scrolling a texture reads the same from every angle the game shows an escalator, and the ramp stays a single static collider. The carry needs ground contact, so a hop still leaves the escalator, and it is also what stops the 0.3-friction ramp from sliding a resting Richie back down — the ramp was always slippery, and until now the only way up was the twenty-four steps. Direction needed care: a box's +Y face maps v backwards along z, so the offset has to rise for the steps to travel upward. Result: a choice at the bottom of the staircase — ride up slowly, or hop the steps and find out how far down they go.

## Iteration 16 — The crowd, at reference quality

Date: 2026-09-19. Goal: "make the humans all hyperrealistic" — the conference crowd held to the bar the robots were held to in iteration 10. AI/tool: Claude Code for the models and the material; ChatGPT image generation for the seven surfaces, driven by Claude Code in the user's own Chrome (prompts/003-people.md): one prompt each, saved from the image viewer, imported with `import-texture.py --tile`. Considered: rigged, scanned-quality human models with animation clips, which would look the part but which the assistant cannot fetch, would add megabytes per character, and would need instancing and level-of-detail work before a hundred and fifty of them ran on a phone. Human decision: rebuild in-engine, with generated textures, so it ships today and stays phone-safe; leave the door open for real models later. Generated: `src/people.ts` rewritten — a lathe torso with shoulders, a waist and hips; a head with a jaw, ears, a nose, eyes in three layers (sclera, iris, pupil) with brows and lids, a mouth, twelve hairstyles as caps of sphere plus whatever the style adds, beards and stubble, glasses with arms, caps and beanies; tee, hoodie (hood up or down), blazer over a shirt, buttoned shirt, dress; tapered limbs with elbows, knees, palms and thumbs, shoes with soles; walkers and guards on jointed legs whose knees fold through the swing, with breathing, a lean into the walk and a head that looks around, or down at a phone. Materials: still one material for the whole crowd, but its map is now a 2048² atlas of sixteen tiles — skin, cotton, denim, fleece, wool, hair, leather, eye — each generated surface painted into its tile and normalised to grey relief so the vertex colour supplies the tone; the atlas doubles as the bump map, and a sixteen-pixel roughness map gives skin its softness and eyes their gloss. Observed problems: none in the build; the embedded browser pane the assistant works in was hidden while it tried to measure the frame rate, so the cost was reasoned rather than measured — a walker went from five draw calls to eight (torso, head, two arms, two thighs, two shins), the audience and the front crowd stay one call per cluster, and vertex counts rose a few times over on geometry that was already tiny. Result: a crowd with faces and knees, in skin with pores, denim with twill, fleece, wool, hair and leather — and a pipeline that swaps any one of them for a better image without touching code.

## Iteration 17 — The real track

Date: 2026-09-22. Goal: replace the placeholder music with the author's own track. AI/tool: Claude Code. Source: **Synaptic Drift** by mITy.John, handed over as a 32 MB WAV master to be converted rather than committed. Generated: `scripts/import-music.py`, which encodes the master to a 128 kbps MP3 (2.9 MB); a rewritten `MusicDirector` that plays it; a credit line on the title screen; `M` as a shortcut; and `src/assets/audio/NOTICE-synaptic-drift.md`. Observed problems: there is no ffmpeg on this machine and pydub needs one, so the conversion runs on `lameenc`, which ships its own LAME and needs nothing else. The track is streamed rather than decoded into an `AudioBuffer` because three minutes of 44.1 kHz stereo is about 65 MB of float32 — a poor trade on a phone for something that only ever plays front to back. An earlier attempt on a branch added a second, parallel music system with its own buttons; iteration 15 had meanwhile built a director with themes and two toggles, so the work was redone on top of it — the class keeps its name, its `menu`/`tutorial`/`game` themes and every call site, and only its insides changed, from scheduling oscillator notes to setting how loud one composed track sits. The theme levels are the "adaptive" part now. Main's first-gesture listener only fired on a `pointerdown` while the title was up, so a player who reached for the keyboard got silence; it now takes the first press of anything. Human decision: the author owns the track and licenses it for this game only, so it sits outside the repository's MIT licence and says so beside the file, in the asset sources and in the README. Result: the game has its own music, credited on the title screen, and the oscillator themes are gone.
